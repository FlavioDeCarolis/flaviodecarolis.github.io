How to read it

Left chart. A curved utility function always lies above its chords, so a sure wealth W beats a fair gamble with the same average. Go sideways from the expected utility to the curve and down to the axis to find the certainty equivalent. The gap between W and that value is the most you would pay to remove the risk.

Right chart. Poorer people lose more utility from a CHF 1,000 loss, so the premium falls as wealth rises. The curve crosses the insurance price at W = 1,250 for ln Y and at W = 2,000 for −1/Y. Below that wealth insurance is a good deal, above it you skip it.

Why the two answers differ. −1/Y bends more than ln Y (relative risk aversion of 2 against 1), so it fears the gamble at higher wealth.

What to notice

The shape of RA(W) gives the class. A horizontal line is CARA, a falling line is DARA and a rising line is IARA. The premium curve has the same shape, because for a small gamble the premium is about ½ · RA · σ².

CRRA is a different question. Relative risk aversion is RR = W · RA. The ln Y function has RR = 1 at every wealth, so it is CRRA, even though its RA falls. Constant RR implies falling RA, but not the other way round.

Why the quadratic is criticised. Its RA rises with wealth, which says that richer people fear a CHF 1,000 loss more. Its utility also peaks at B, so beyond that wealth more money makes the agent worse off.

Gamble versus certainty equivalent

Move the wealth slider. The gamble is ±CHF 1,000 at equal odds, so its expected value is your wealth W. The certainty equivalent (CEQ) is the sure amount that gives the same utility as the gamble. All three agents have the same risk aversion at W = 2,000, so any difference you see at other wealth levels comes from the class alone. The second slider sets how risk averse they all are at that point.

Where each agent's certainty equivalent falls

Each line runs from the bad outcome to the good outcome of the gamble. The shaded part is the risk premium: what the agent gives up to get rid of the risk. It stays the same for CARA, shrinks for DARA and grows for IARA as W rises.

Problem 3

Can you rank two risky investments without knowing the investor?

Investment A pays 2, 4 or 9 with probabilities 0.25, 0.5 and 0.25. Investment B pays 1, 6 or 8 with probability one third each. Each criterion assumes more about the investor than the one before, so each can rank fewer pairs.

First-order dominance

Assumes only that the investor prefers more to less. A beats B when FA(x) ≤ FB(x) for every x, so A is at least as likely as B to clear any threshold.

A and B: no ranking

The CDFs cross, so some payoff levels favour A and others favour B.

Second-order dominance

Assumes the investor is also risk averse. A beats B when the area under FA never exceeds the area under FB. Then every risk-averse investor picks A.

A and B: no ranking

The areas cross at x = 5.2, and B has the higher mean.

Mean-variance

Assumes the investor cares only about the mean and the variance. A beats B when its mean is at least as high and its variance at most as high.

A and B: no ranking

B has the higher mean and the higher variance. The answer depends on how much variance is disliked.

The numbers behind the charts

D(x) is the area under FB minus the area under FA. A positive sign favours A. The signs of FB − FA and of D both change along the row, so neither FSD nor SSD holds.

How to read it

The three criteria form a ladder. First-order dominance implies second-order dominance, which implies a higher mean. Each rung is easier to satisfy but holds for fewer investors. Here both dominance tests fail, and mean-variance is inconclusive.

Why the CDFs cross. A has the better worst case (2 against 1) and the better best case (9 against 8). B wins in the middle: it pays 6 where A typically pays 4. That middle outcome is what gives B its higher mean.

What a partial ordering means. The criteria never say that two investments are equally good. They say that every investor in a group agrees, or that the group is split. Here the risk-averse group is split.

Problem 4

Why lower variance does not mean second-order dominance

Part (a) shows that when two investments have the same mean, second-order dominance implies lower variance. Part (b) shows that the reverse fails. The key is what variance measures and what second-order dominance demands.

Part (a): a spread adds variance

A takes 2 or 4 at equal odds. B is A plus zero-mean noise ε: every outcome of A moves up or down by s at equal odds. The mean stays at 3, the outcomes move apart, and the variance grows by exactly s². Every risk-averse agent dislikes this.

Part (b): lower variance does not imply second-order dominance

L1 pays 0, 2, 4 or 6 with probability ¼ each. L2 pays 0.1, 3 or 5.9 with probability ⅓ each. The agent has utility U(Y) = 2Y for Y ≤ 3 and 3 + Y for Y > 3. If lower variance implied SSD, L1 would dominate L2 and every risk-averse agent would prefer L1. So it is enough to find one risk-averse agent who prefers L2.

Step 4: conclusion

L1 has the lower variance, yet this risk-averse agent prefers L2. Second-order dominance means that every risk-averse agent prefers the dominating lottery. So L1 does not second-order dominate L2, even though the two have the same mean and L1 has the lower variance.

L2 does not dominate L1 either. By part (a), a dominating lottery with equal mean must have the lower variance, and L2 has the higher one.

The intuition in one picture

Variance counts every move away from 3, up or down. The agent in Step 1 only worries about falling below 3, and each franc lost there costs twice as much as a franc gained above 3 is worth. Shaded bars show how far each outcome falls below 3.

L2 has the larger variance (5.61 against 5.00). But the average fall below 3, which is what this agent fears, is a little smaller for L2: ⅓ × 2.9 = 0.97 against ¼ × 3 + ¼ × 1 = 1.00. The two measures look at different things, so they can rank the lotteries differently. Second-order dominance asks that every risk-averse agent agrees, and here they do not.
