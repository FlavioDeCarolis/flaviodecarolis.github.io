Hormuz, the Rhine, and the Price of Location

Geopolitical and climate risks are shaping investors' decisions today. The World Economic Forum ranks geoeconomic confrontation as the top short-term global risk for 2026, with extreme weather among the most severe over every horizon.1 By geopolitical risk we mean the risk of wars, terrorist attacks, and oil shocks. Climate risk usually comes in two forms: transition risk, the risk to firms as the economy moves away from carbon, and physical risk, the risk that extreme weather or longer-term shifts in water supply or sea level hit a firm's sites.2 In 2026, both are elevated at once: several ongoing wars keep geopolitical risk high, while an exceptional year of drought and extreme weather has driven up physical climate risk.

Under these conditions, a firm's location decides how exposed it is, and Switzerland is a clear example. The Swiss National Bank3 reports that Swiss inflation rose from 0.1% in February 2026 to 0.6% in May, and attributes the rise mainly to higher prices for imported oil products. The geopolitical side of the shock traces back to oil in the Middle East: the bank draws on direct conversations with company managers, who say that the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has raised their purchase costs. Energy and transport for imported inputs have become more expensive.

The climate side of the shock runs through a river, the Rhine, which is running low this year. The Central Commission for the Navigation of the Rhine documents how low water lifts freight rates, through low-water surcharges and smaller loads,4 and S&P Global Commodity Insights5 shows how low levels reroute the physical flow of oil into the European interior. The effect is regional, not continental. The Rhine carries a large share of the fuel Switzerland imports, according to the Swiss Federal Statistical Office,6 and the extra cost reaches the pump inland, where the Touring Club Suisse tracks retail prices in near real time.7

This location risk is not only a European river problem. On 10 and 11 September 2026, drones launched from Iraq struck Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the 1,200-kilometre line that moves crude from the eastern oil fields to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. Saudi Arabia shut the line as a precaution.8 The strike mattered because the pipeline had become the main route around the Strait of Hormuz. A bypass built to avoid one chokepoint had itself become a chokepoint. As global trade concentrates into a small number of straits, pipelines, and rivers, each remaining route carries more volume and more risk. The firm at the end of that route pays for it in higher delivered costs.

From fuel prices, the story now moves to firms — and to a blind spot for investors. Whether a company sits at the end of a vulnerable route depends on where its plants, storage, and shipping points actually are. Yet that location data is often missing, incomplete, or untracked over time across much of the world, even though investors need exactly this data to measure exposure.9 I aim to close that gap by linking firms to their physical sites, and linking those sites to the climate and geopolitical risks they face. Where a company produces and ships its goods affects both its competitiveness and its margins. These shocks are a reminder that, for an investor, a firm's location is not just an address. It is part of its risk, and part of its value.
