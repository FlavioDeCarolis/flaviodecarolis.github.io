A Database That Checks What Net-Zero Pledges Don't Say

A net-zero pledge means little if investors cannot see what the company is actually building. That risk is easier to check now, because a small team chose to spend years building the Global Ethylene Production Database (GEPD) and making it publicly accessible.

In this post, I publish parts of an interview with Christophe Christiaen, Head of the Spatial Finance Initiative at the Oxford Sustainable Finance Group and Smith School of Enterprise and the Environment, on how investors can use the GEPD to test petrochemical companies against their net-zero pledges. The database shows how much carbon is locked into the sector's committed production, and how investors can use those numbers today to test a company's transition credibility. Christophe was candid about what building the database took: uneasy collaboration between social scientists and machine learning engineers learning to trust each other's work, and a validation process he called fatiguing even with AI's help. As he put it, "everybody likes the dataset, nobody wants to build it." He and his colleagues built the database anyway, and it will live on through Global Energy Monitor's trackers. Thank you to Christophe for walking me through the dataset in such detail.

Why the GEPD Matters, and the Connection to My Last Post

My previous post traced how a firm's location decides its exposure to shocks like the closure of the Strait of Hormuz or a low Rhine.1 That post ended on an admission: location data for individual firms is often missing. The same blind spot applies to a harder question. Not just where a company operates, but what it is building there, and how much carbon that plant locks in for decades.

The GEPD shows why the location gap matters. Ethylene production alone could consume more than 30% of the entire chemical sector's remaining carbon budget for staying under 1.5°C, starting now.2 Reported emissions do not map cleanly onto what companies actually produce, so forward-looking committed emissions come out higher than current pledges suggest.

Christophe Christiaen and his co-authors built a new asset-level map to answer that question for one industry: petrochemicals.2 Their GEPD covers 342 facilities worldwide, plant by plant, owner by owner. The database tracks ethylene crackers, a highly capital-intensive unit that turns oil and gas into the building block of most plastics.

Christophe told me this work is part of a multi-industry global mapping effort that has been running since 2020. Early attempts to automate data collection produced limited results and still needed heavy manual checking. Even two years ago, with the advent of large language models, AI-assisted extraction hallucinated badly and still required significant manual validation. Only recently has that kind of automated extraction become reliable enough to deploy at scale. Building the GEPD took years of iteration and largely manual work, and the team has now partnered with the NGO Global Energy Monitor on a Global Chemicals Inventory to keep the information up to date.

What the Data Can Test Today

I asked Christophe what an investor could do with the database. His answer was concrete: "If a company owns a large ethylene asset, is it still economically viable to run it at current gas and LNG prices? If a company claims transition credibility while continuing to invest in this kind of asset, that claim can now be checked directly against the asset itself."

He named two further examples in the interview. On China, Christophe's point was narrower: "the sector's current oversupply makes a company's Chinese ethylene exposure informative about its competitiveness." The database itself shows the scale of that exposure: 17,200 kilotonnes of new ethylene capacity already under construction in China, more than in any other country.2

On the Middle East, Christophe pointed to how much current capacity sits there, tied to trade routes through the Strait of Hormuz. The database's own figures show why that matters: Saudi Arabia holds 8% of global ethylene capacity and Iran another 5%, much of it moved through or around the strait, including the same pipeline I covered in my last post.2 Both risks, competitive and geopolitical, sit inside the same asset-level data.

My View on the GEPD and What It Means for Practitioners

Use the GEPD's information on continued investment in a specific carbon-locking asset as a direct, checkable challenge to a company's transition plan. Use the database to benchmark peers within a sector: who is expanding into overcapacity, and who is aligned with what they have pledged. Use its geographic data to assess physical, geopolitical and transition exposure together. This generation of data is built for scrutiny and engagement with companies.

It is tempting to connect the database directly to portfolio construction methods, such as Bolton, Kacperczyk and Samama's 2022 approach to building low-tracking-error portfolios against a carbon budget.3 The database covers one product in one industry, though, so it lacks the full cross-sector universe a portfolio-level carbon budget needs. Broader, cross-sector coverage would be a useful feature for future databases.

This post is analysis, not investment advice.
